20 AI Concepts Explained Simply

Nov 20, 2026 · by Rezwan Hossain

Everyone talks about tokens, RAG, agents and MCP, but most explanations either stay too vague or jump straight into math. This post sits in the middle.

Each idea gets three things: an everyday picture to hang it on, a short plain explanation, and a small animation. At the end of each one there's a single line to remember. Read them in order, because each idea builds on the one before it.

Part 1: How a Model Reads and Learns

1. Large Language Model (LLM)

Like: the autocomplete on your phone, if it had read a huge part of the internet.

An LLM is a program that guesses the next word. You give it some text, it picks the most likely next piece, adds it, and guesses again. Repeat that a few thousand times and you get an email, an essay or a block of code.

"Large" means it has billions of numbers inside (called parameters) that were tuned while it read a huge amount of text. ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Llama are all LLMs. It doesn't look facts up. It writes what sounds most likely, which is why it can be wrong with full confidence.

The cat sat on the mat

mat62%
sofa21%
roof9%

it scores every possible next word, then picks one

LLM = a next-word guesser, repeated very fast.

2. Tokenization

Like: chopping vegetables before cooking. The model can't take a whole sentence in one bite. It needs small pieces.

Models don't see letters or words. They see tokens. Common words stay whole ("the", "cat"), while rare words get cut into parts ("un" + "believ" + "able"). Each token is then swapped for a number from a fixed list called the vocabulary.

In English, one token is roughly 4 characters, or about ¾ of a word. This matters because prices and limits are counted in tokens. It also explains some odd mistakes. A model can miscount the letters in "strawberry" because it sees chunks, not letters.

un359 believ14361 able481 !0

one word, four tokens, four numbers (example IDs)

Tokens are the model's letters. You pay per token.

3. Vectorization (Embeddings)

Like: GPS coordinates for meaning. Places close on a map are near each other, and words with close meanings get close numbers.

Each token becomes a long list of numbers called a vector (or embedding), often hundreds or thousands of numbers long. These numbers are learned so that "cat" and "kitten" land near each other and far from "truck".

Now the computer can do math with meaning. It can measure how close two ideas are. The famous example: king − man + woman lands close to queen.

Vector = meaning turned into a point on a map.

4. Attention

Like: a noisy party. Twenty people are talking, but when someone says your name, you tune in to that one voice.

When the model reads a word, attention lets it look back at every other word and decide which ones matter for this one. In "The animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired", the word "it" should look hard at "animal", not "street".

Each word gives every other word a score, and the high scores shape its meaning. Change "tired" to "wide" and "it" now points to "street".

The animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired

darker green = "it" pays more attention to that word

Attention = each word asks "which words matter to me?"

5. Self-Supervised Learning

Like: covering a word in a book with your thumb, guessing it, then lifting your thumb to check. No teacher needed, because the book is the answer key.

Training needs a lot of examples with correct answers, and writing billions of them by hand is impossible. The trick is to hide the next word of real text and make the model guess it. The real word is the right answer, so any text becomes free training data.

Wrong guess? The model's numbers get nudged a tiny bit. Repeat that trillions of times. This first big stage is called pre-training.

The sky is blue

The capital of France is Paris

If it rains, the ground gets wet

hide a word, guess, check. The text grades itself

The data labels itself: hide a word, guess it, check.

6. Transformer

Like: an office building where each floor reads the draft, adds notes and sends it up. By the top floor, the meaning is very rich.

The transformer is the design behind almost every modern LLM. It came from a 2017 Google paper called "Attention Is All You Need". Tokens enter at the bottom as vectors. Each layer does two jobs: attention (words look at each other), then a small feed-forward network (each word thinks on its own).

Stack dozens of layers, and the top one gives scores for the next token. The big win is that it reads all the words at once instead of one by one, so it trains fast on GPUs. The "T" in GPT stands for Transformer.

Part 2: Making a Model Useful

7. Fine-tuning

Like: a general doctor training to be a heart specialist. They keep what they knew and get extra practice in one area.

A pre-trained model knows a little about everything. Fine-tuning trains it some more on a smaller, focused set of examples, like your company's support chats, legal Q&As or code in your style.

The chat assistants you use were fine-tuned too, on "question → good answer" examples. That's what turned a text continuer into a helper. Cheaper methods like LoRA train only a small add-on instead of every number in the model.

base model
general legal expert

Fine-tuning = extra classes in one subject.

8. Few-shot Prompting

Like: showing a new co-worker three filled-in forms before asking them to fill in the fourth.

Instead of retraining, you put a few examples right in your prompt. The model spots the pattern and follows it. No examples is zero-shot, one is one-shot, and a handful is few-shot.

It's the quickest way to get a fixed format, tone or set of labels. Nothing inside the model changes, and it forgets the examples after the reply.

"Loved every bite" → positive

"Waste of money" → negative

"Best pizza in town" → positive

"Cold and late" → negative

three examples in, the model copies the pattern

Show, don't tell: put 2 to 5 examples in the prompt.

9. Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG)

Like: an open-book exam. Instead of answering from memory, the student first finds the right page.

An LLM only knows what it saw in training. It doesn't know last week's news or your company's documents. RAG fixes that in three steps:

  1. Retrieve: search your documents for parts related to the question.
  2. Augment: paste those parts into the prompt.
  3. Generate: the model answers using them and can say where the answer came from.

You get fresher, more accurate answers without retraining anything.

What is our refund window?

pricing.md refund-policy.md team.md faq.md roadmap.md

prompt: question + 2 found parts

answer: 30 days from purchase (source: refund-policy.md)

RAG = look it up first, then answer.

10. Vector Database

Like: a library sorted by meaning instead of by title. Books about the same idea sit on the same shelf, even if they use different words.

RAG has to find related text fast. A vector database stores the embedding (from concept 3) of every chunk of your documents. Your question is turned into a vector too, and the database returns the nearest ones, the top k.

So "how do I get my money back?" finds the "refund policy" chunk even though they share no words. Popular options are pgvector (inside Postgres), Pinecone, Qdrant, Chroma and Weaviate.

your question refunds returns billing hiring logo office wifi roadmap

search = "give me the 3 closest points"

Vector DB = search by meaning, not by keyword.

11. Model Context Protocol (MCP)

Like: USB-C for AI. One port shape, and any charger, screen or drive just works.

Before MCP, every AI app needed its own custom code for every tool: one for GitHub, one for Slack, one for your database. MCP is an open standard, released by Anthropic in November 2024, that gives them one shared way to connect.

A tool is wrapped once as an MCP server, and any AI app that speaks MCP (the client) can use it. A server can offer tools (actions, like "create an issue"), resources (data to read, like files) and prompts (ready-made templates).

12. Context Engineering

Like: packing a small suitcase for a trip. You can't take everything, so you pick exactly what you'll need.

The context window is everything the model sees for one reply: instructions, chat history, documents and tool results. It has a size limit, and extra noise makes answers worse.

Prompt engineering is about how you word the question. Context engineering is the bigger job of choosing what goes in: the right documents, a short summary of old chat instead of the full log, only the tools that apply. Then you leave the rest out. The term caught on in 2025, helped along by Andrej Karpathy.

system rules

user's question

Not more context. The right context.

Part 3: Acting and Thinking

13. Agents

Like: the difference between a friend who tells you how to book a flight and an assistant who actually books it.

An agent is an LLM that works in a loop with tools. It gets a goal, thinks of a next step, uses a tool (search the web, run code, call an API), looks at the result and decides what to do next. It keeps going until the job is done.

A chatbot answers. An agent acts. Many agents get their tools through MCP.

think→ act→ observe→ repeat

search flights to Chittagong

check calendar for Friday

book the cheapest seat

Agent = LLM + tools + a loop.

14. Reinforcement Learning

Like: training a dog with treats. No one explains the rules. Good moves get a treat, bad moves don't.

The model tries something, gets a reward score, and slowly shifts toward actions that score higher. For chat models, people compare two answers and pick the better one. That feedback trains a "reward model", and RL pushes the LLM toward answers people prefer. This is called RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback).

Newer models also get rewards from results you can check, like whether the math answer is right or the code passes its tests.

Try, get a score, do more of what scored well.

15. Chain of Thought

Like: showing your work in math class. Writing each step makes you less likely to slip.

Chain of thought means getting the model to reason step by step before the final answer. Each new token is based on the ones before it, so writing out the steps gives the model room to think. This makes math and logic answers much better. In early tests, just adding "Let's think step by step" to a prompt was enough to help.

Try this one: a pen and a notebook cost $1.10 together. The notebook costs $1.00 more than the pen. How much is the pen?

answer right away

pen = 10¢ ✗

step by step

pen = x

notebook = x + 1.00

2x + 1.00 = 1.10

pen = 5¢ ✓

Steps first, answer last.

16. Reasoning Models

Like: a chess player who thinks through moves before touching a piece, compared with one who plays the first move that looks good.

Reasoning models, like OpenAI's o-series, DeepSeek-R1 and the "thinking" modes in Claude and Gemini, are trained (mostly with RL from concept 14) to write a long chain of thought on their own before answering. They try an idea, check it and fix their mistakes.

They are slower and use more tokens, but they're much better at math, code and multi-step problems. For "what's the capital of France?", they're overkill.

thinking

maybe the pen is 10¢?

then notebook = $1.10, total $1.20… no

let pen = x, notebook = x + 1

2x + 1 = 1.10, so x = 0.05

check: 0.05 + 1.05 = 1.10 ✓

answer: the pen costs 5¢

Reasoning model = chain of thought built in, trained by RL.

Part 4: Shapes and Sizes

17. Multi-modal Models

Like: a person who can read, look and listen, not only read.

A multi-modal model takes in more than text: images, audio and sometimes video. Some can also produce images or speech. Each kind of input is turned into vectors in the same space, so the model can reason across them.

You can send a screenshot and ask "what's wrong with this chart?", or ask a spoken question about a photo.

Many senses, one brain.

18. Small Language Models (SLMs)

Like: a pocket dictionary compared with a 30-volume encyclopedia. It doesn't know everything, but it's fast and fits in your bag.

Small models have from a few hundred million to a few billion parameters, like Phi, Gemma, Llama 3.2 (1B and 3B) and the small Qwen models. They run on a laptop or phone, work offline, keep your data on your device and cost far less.

They're great at focused jobs like sorting messages, autocomplete or simple chat, especially after fine-tuning.

Small model = fast, cheap, private, good at one thing.

19. Distillation

Like: a top chef training an apprentice. The apprentice learns not only the dish but the chef's judgment.

Distillation trains a small student model to copy a big teacher. The student doesn't only learn the right answer. It learns the teacher's full set of guesses, like "70% cat, 20% fox, 10% dog". Those numbers carry extra hints, because a fox looks a bit like a cat.

The result is a small model that keeps much of the big one's skill. Many small models are made this way. Geoffrey Hinton's 2015 paper made the idea popular.

Big teacher, small student. Copy the confidence, not just the answer.

20. Quantization

Like: rounding prices. $4.9873 becomes $4.99. It's a little less exact, but much easier to handle.

A model's parameters are numbers, usually stored with 16 or 32 bits each. Quantization stores them with fewer bits, often 8 or 4. A model with 7 billion parameters needs about 28 GB at 32-bit, 14 GB at 16-bit, 7 GB at 8-bit and 3.5 GB at 4-bit.

It runs faster and fits on normal hardware, with a small drop in quality. That's how people run models on their own computer with tools like llama.cpp and Ollama.

0.73519842 32-bit · 28 GB 0.7352 16-bit · 14 GB 0.735 8-bit · 7 GB 0.73 4-bit · 3.5 GB

size of a 7B model. Less detail per number, much smaller file

Fewer bits per number = smaller, faster model.

How It All Fits Together

Here's what happens when you ask an AI assistant a question:

  1. Your text is cut into tokens, and each becomes a vector.
  2. A transformer uses attention to work out what each word means in context.
  3. It was pre-trained by guessing hidden words, then fine-tuned and polished with RL to act as a helpful assistant.
  4. Before it answers, the app does context engineering: it may use RAG to pull your docs from a vector database, add few-shot examples and connect tools through MCP.
  5. An agent can loop: think, call a tool, check the result, repeat. A reasoning model does a long chain of thought first.
  6. To run it on a phone, you take a small model, often made by distillation, and shrink it with quantization.
